SSL/TLS Website Security Explained for Business Owners

What every business owner needs to know about SSL certificates, TLS configuration, and encryption — without the technical jargon.

Published: May 8, 2026 · Reading time: 9 minutes

You have probably heard that your website needs an SSL certificate. You may even have one installed. But there is a big difference between having a certificate and having proper SSL/TLS website security. Many business owners install a certificate and never look at it again — not realizing that their encryption settings may be weak, misconfigured, or about to expire.

What Are SSL and TLS?

SSL (Secure Sockets Layer) and TLS (Transport Layer Security) are protocols that encrypt the connection between a visitor's browser and your web server. When someone visits your website, SSL/TLS ensures that the data exchanged — including form submissions, login credentials, and browsing activity — cannot be read by anyone intercepting the connection.

Technically, SSL is the older protocol and has been replaced by TLS. But the term "SSL certificate" is still widely used even though modern certificates actually use TLS. When we talk about an SSL security check, we are really talking about verifying that your TLS configuration is secure.

Why SSL/TLS Matters for Business Owners

Beyond the obvious security benefits, SSL/TLS affects your business in several ways:

  • Browser warnings: Chrome, Safari, and Firefox now display "Not Secure" warnings for sites without HTTPS. This instantly erodes visitor trust.
  • SEO rankings: Google uses HTTPS as a ranking signal. Sites with proper SSL/TLS may rank higher than identical sites without it.
  • Compliance: PCI-DSS (for payment processing), HIPAA, and GDPR all require encrypted connections for sensitive data.
  • Data protection: Login credentials, contact form data, and customer information are encrypted in transit.

Common SSL/TLS Problems We Find

1. Expired Certificates

It sounds basic, but expired certificates are still a leading cause of website outages and browser warnings. Let's Encrypt certificates expire after 90 days. If your auto-renewal fails for any reason, your certificate expires and your site shows security warnings to every visitor. An SSL certificate audit catches this before it becomes a problem.

2. Weak TLS Configuration

Having a valid certificate is not enough if your server supports outdated and insecure protocols. TLS 1.0 and TLS 1.1 are now considered insecure and should be disabled. TLS 1.2 should be the minimum, with TLS 1.3 preferred for modern browsers. We frequently find servers that still allow TLS 1.0 connections, leaving them vulnerable to downgrade attacks.

3. Weak Cipher Suites

Cipher suites are the algorithms used to encrypt the connection. Some older cipher suites have known vulnerabilities (like RC4) or use inadequate key lengths. A proper TLS configuration uses only strong, modern cipher suites. Our audits check every cipher your server offers and flags the weak ones.

4. Certificate Chain Issues

An SSL certificate is part of a chain — your site certificate is signed by an intermediate certificate, which is signed by a root certificate. If your server is not configured to serve the complete chain, some browsers and devices may not trust your certificate. This is a common misconfiguration that causes intermittent errors on mobile devices and older systems.

5. Mixed Content

Mixed content occurs when your HTTPS page loads some resources (images, scripts, stylesheets) over HTTP. Browsers will block some of these resources or display warnings, breaking your page and eroding trust. This often happens after migrating a site from HTTP to HTTPS without updating all internal links.

6. Missing HSTS (HTTP Strict Transport Security)

Even with a valid certificate, visitors who type "yourwebsite.com" (without HTTPS) are initially connected over HTTP before being redirected. This brief HTTP connection is an opportunity for a man-in-the-middle attack. HSTS tells browsers to connect only over HTTPS, eliminating this window of vulnerability.

How We Test SSL/TLS Security

Our website audits use SSLyze and testssl.sh to perform deep TLS analysis. These tools check:

  • Certificate validity, expiry date, and chain completeness
  • Supported TLS versions and which ones should be disabled
  • Cipher suite strength and which ones are vulnerable
  • HSTS configuration and max-age value
  • Certificate Transparency log presence
  • OCSP stapling configuration

The raw scan data from these tools is included in your audit report, so you can see exactly what a browser sees when it connects to your site.

What Is a Good TLS Configuration?

A well-configured TLS setup in 2026 should:

  • Support only TLS 1.2 and TLS 1.3 (disable TLS 1.0 and 1.1)
  • Prefer TLS 1.3 ciphers where supported
  • Use a certificate from a trusted Certificate Authority
  • Have HSTS enabled with a max-age of at least one year
  • Serve the complete certificate chain
  • Have OCSP stapling enabled for improved performance and privacy

Do Not Assume Your SSL Is Fine

The majority of small business websites we scan have at least one SSL/TLS issue. Often it is something the site owner did not even know to check — like a weak cipher suite or a missing intermediate certificate. A proper SSL certificate audit gives you the complete picture.

Remember: a padlock icon in the browser address bar only means the certificate is valid. It does not mean the encryption is strong, the configuration is secure, or the certificate will not expire next week.
